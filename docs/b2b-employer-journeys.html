01

The premise everything else hangs off

Every CV screener on the market ranks candidates. Almost none of them can tell a hiring manager why, in the candidate's own words, and almost none of them survive a data protection officer reading the model card. Tailr can do both today — not as a roadmap item, but as code that already runs in production.

The tailor pipeline already produces, for any CV against any job description, a list of 6–12 typed requirements — each marked must or nice, each graded strong / transferable / partial / none, each carrying a quoted line from the CV as its evidence. computeMatchScore then turns that into a number with plain arithmetic: musts weigh double, and the four strengths are worth 1, 0.6, 0.25 and 0. No model is asked for a score. The same inputs give the same number every time, and every point of it traces back to a sentence a person wrote about themselves.

That is the product. The recruiter side is not a new AI — it is the existing assessment, pointed at many candidates instead of one, and wrapped in the governance an employer needs before they are allowed to use it.

Law 1 · already ours

Tailr reframes evidence; it never manufactures it. Empty beats invented.

Decided 28 July, enforced in the tailor prompts, Career Arc extraction and loadProvenSkills. It exists because we cannot sell recruiters a tool for spotting inflated CVs while selling candidates a tool that inflates them. On the employer side it becomes the sales pitch: a score you can put in front of a rejected candidate.

Law 2 · proposed here

Tailr ranks and evidences. A person decides. Nothing is ever auto-rejected.

Not a setting, not a plan tier — a property of the product, like Law 1. It is what gets us past a DPO in week two rather than week ten, it is what keeps us out of the worst of the EU AI Act's Annex III employment obligations, and it is the reason a hiring manager can defend the shortlist in a debrief. This is decision 1 for you.

02

Process map A — the operating loop

One requisition, start to finish. This is what a customer does every week, and it is the map the five persona flows all attach to. Read it first: the commercial process exists only to get an organisation to the point where this loop runs.

03

Process map B — the commercial lifecycle

How an organisation gets from "we drown in applications" to running the loop above. Five phases, four gates. The gates matter more than the phases: each one is a place we should be willing to walk away, and the back-test in phase three is the one that decides whether we have a business here at all.

What each gate actually asks

1
Is there enough volume for a ranking to be worth anything?
Roughly 150+ applications per requisition, an ATS we can export from, and a named person who owns data protection. Below that, a recruiter reading every CV is genuinely fine and we should say so.
2
Will their DPO accept how this works?
Law 2 accepted in writing, retention period agreed, sub-processors (Anthropic, Supabase, Vercel) accepted, candidate-facing notice drafted. No pilot before this. Not a shortcut we take for a logo.
3
Did it agree with their own judgement — on a req where we already know the answer?
Bar set before the pilot runs, not negotiated after. Suggested: Tailr's top 20 contains at least 4 of the 5 people they actually interviewed, and surfaces at least one strong candidate they missed. If we miss the bar we say so and keep the finding.
4
Is anyone actually using it two weeks after onboarding?
Three recruiters, real requisitions, unprompted. An unused seat is worse than a lost deal — it is a reference we can never use.

Each gets a journey — what they do, what they think, where it breaks, what Tailr does about it — and the flow they walk. Tailr's own side (you selling, then supporting) is the top lane of process map B rather than a sixth journey.

05

What we already have, and what this costs

Sized against the repo as it stands on 9 September. The assessment engine is the expensive part and it is done. Almost everything left is organisational plumbing and governance surfaces — unglamorous, but not research.

Already built, reusable as-is

  • Requirement extraction — 6–12 typed must/nice requirements with keywords, straight out of the tailor pipeline.
  • Evidence mapping — per-requirement strength plus a quoted line from the CV, with "empty beats invented" enforced in the prompts.
  • Deterministic scoring — computeMatchScore: musts weigh double, four strength values, reproducible and explainable.
  • JD ingest from any URL — bare hosts normalised, works across job boards.
  • CV parsing — PDF, DOCX and TXT, already in production.
  • Gap-probing interview questions — the hiring manager's prep, already generated.
  • Auth, rate limiting, row-level security — the whole Supabase foundation.
  • A read-only viewer role — the pattern Sam's audit seat should copy.
  • Career Path — where a rejected candidate's gaps go. The other half of the loop.

Net new for the employer side

  • Organisations, seats and roles — everything today is a single user with a single subscription. This is the real schema work.
  • Requisition as an object — a JD, a calibrated requirement set, and many candidates against it.
  • Data separation — an employer's candidate data must never touch consumer Career Path data. RLS design, decided before any code.
  • ATS integration — candidate sync. Marcus will not re-upload CVs by hand and the whole thing dies if we ask him to.
  • The audit log — immutable, queryable by the customer, the artefact gate 2 turns on.
  • Candidate notice + consent surface — shown at application, on the employer's side.
  • Bias monitoring — outcome rates by group, or Sam cannot renew.
  • Recruiter review UI — the ranked pile and the evidence card. Figma first, per the working rules.
  • Feedback-to-candidate surface — gated on decision 4 below.
06

Six decisions I need from you

Each of these changes the maps above. I have given a recommendation on all six rather than a menu — argue with the recommendation, that is faster than starting from nothing.

1. Is "no auto-reject" a product law or a configurable setting?

Every gate, every persona journey and the entire compliance story on this page assumes it is a law. Making it configurable would let us sell to high-volume employers who want the pile cut automatically — and would collapse the candidate feedback loop, the Article 22 answer, and Sam's approval in the same move.

RecommendationA law, written next to the Evidence Rule in docs/ and enforced in code, not policy. It is the cheapest competitive moat we will ever get, and we cannot un-ring it later.

2. Does the employer product ever read a candidate's own Tailr profile?

If a candidate has a Career Arc with us, we know far more than their submitted CV. Using it without their explicit, per-employer consent would be a serious breach of trust and probably of law. Not using it means we assess employers' candidates from the CV alone, like everyone else.

RecommendationNever by default. Candidate-initiated only: they choose to attach their Arc to a specific application, and can see exactly what the employer sees. Anything else poisons the consumer product.

3. Where does it live — inside the ATS, or as its own surface?

Marcus lives in the ATS. Dana lives in email. Priya lives in a dashboard. A standalone app is far faster to build and is the thing Marcus stops opening in week three, failing gate 4.

RecommendationOwn surface at app.gettailr.com/hire for the pilot, with CSV import in and out — but treat one real ATS integration (Greenhouse or Workable) as the price of leaving pilot, not as a phase-two nice-to-have.

4. Is candidate feedback opt-in for employers, on by default, or mandatory?

The strategic hinge, and the diamond on Ade's flow. Mandatory feedback makes Tailr the only screener a candidate would want an employer to use — and will lose deals with legal teams who see any feedback as litigation risk.

RecommendationOn by default, employer can turn it off, and we say plainly in the sales conversation that we would rather they did not. Mandatory in the pilot cohort, so we learn whether it actually causes the problem legal teams fear.

5. What do we charge for, and who signs?

Per seat rewards us for recruiters logging in, which is not the value. Per requisition matches the unit of work. Per candidate screened scales with the pile but prices us out of exactly the high-volume grad reqs that are our best case.

RecommendationPer requisition, with an annual commitment and unlimited candidates inside it. It matches the loop in process map A, it rewards the outcome rather than the login, and it makes the back-test pilot trivially priceable.

6. Does this fork the recruitment prototype, or replace it?

The prototype scored candidates on evidence strength (25%) and confidence (10%), and is paused pending Y's feedback. These maps assume a different scoring basis — the production tailor pipeline's requirement mapping — because that one already exists and can show its receipts.

RecommendationReplace the scoring model; keep whatever the prototype learned about recruiter workflow. Two scoring systems for the same judgement is exactly the contradiction the Evidence Rule was written to prevent.

Two things to flag on process, per the working rules.
