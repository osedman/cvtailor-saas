The headlines say your job is over

Every few months a report lands announcing the end of some line of work. This month it was coders being told to consider retraining as electricians and nurses. Last year it was copywriters. Before that, support agents. If you work with a keyboard, someone has recently predicted your extinction, in confident language, with a chart.

Two responses did the rounds this week and they are worth more than the report. The first came from a grey-haired developer who started on mainframes: he has watched this exact panic before. In the nineties, business users told him to his face that they would not need developers soon, because a new tool let them build everything themselves. Five years later he was busier than ever. The panic was real. The prediction was not.

The second response did something simpler. It lined the confident predictions up against the predictors' own job adverts, and found the people announcing the end of a profession were hiring furiously for it, with open roles more than tripling over the same year. Whatever a press release says, a job posting is a company spending money on a belief. Watch the second one.

What actually helps

Read the hiring, not the headline

When anyone forecasts the death of a job, check whether they are recruiting for it. Postings, budgets and contracts are what a company actually believes. Predictions are what it wants said about itself. When the two disagree, the money is telling the truth.

Do not pivot on a headline

Retraining wholesale in a panic means selling years of built skill at its lowest ever price, on the strength of a scenario. If a change of direction is right for you, it will still be right after a calm look at real postings. If it only makes sense while you are frightened, it is not a plan.

Argue from tasks, not titles

What actually shifts in these waves is tasks inside jobs, not whole careers overnight. Titles get renamed; the work moves around. A CV built on evidenced capabilities survives a relabelling. A CV built on a job title is hostage to whether that title stays fashionable.

Protections are now a thing you can ask about

This week nearly two thousand games developers signed a contract requiring any AI rollout that affects their work to be negotiated first, with layoff notice and rehiring rights attached. You may not have a union, but you can ask any employer what their approach is. The answer, or the flinch, is information.

Do the boring check instead

An hour of doom scrolling tells you nothing about your own position. An hour against three real postings tells you exactly which requirements you already cover and which gaps are worth closing. Fear is general. Your situation is specific, and specific is workable.

A press release is a belief. A job posting is money behind one.
