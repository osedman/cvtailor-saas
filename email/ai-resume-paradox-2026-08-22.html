They screen with AI, then reject you for using it

This week the job-hunting corners of Reddit circled one uncomfortable paradox. Most companies now use AI to read your resume, yet half of hiring managers say they bin anything that reads like AI wrote it. Here is what the community is seeing, and how to stay on the right side of both machines and humans.

The paradox, in two numbers

Around 82% of companies now use AI to review resumes, while 49% of hiring managers say they automatically dismiss resumes that read as AI-written. Candidates are understandably fed up: the same employers automating their side of the process are penalising applicants for doing the same thing. But the rejection is real, and it is worth understanding what actually triggers it.

Recruiters are drowning in identical applications

Recruiters describe hundreds of applications per role where the phrasing is eerily similar, because thousands of people are feeding the same job posting into the same tools with the same prompts. The result is a wall of polished, interchangeable text. In that pile, sounding like everyone else is what gets you skipped, not the fact that a tool was involved.

What actually reads as "AI slop"

The consensus from recruiters in these threads: it is not tool use they can detect, it is emptiness. Buzzword soup, claims with no numbers, achievements that could belong to anyone, and bullets that mirror the job ad word for word without evidence behind them. A resume grounded in your real, specific experience passes as human because it is. Generic output fails because it says nothing only you could say.

What to actually do this week

1. Start from your real CV, not a blank prompt. Tools should reshape what you actually did, never invent what sounds good.
2. Put a number in every bullet you can. Specific figures are the fastest human-written signal there is, and they are the first thing generic AI text lacks.
3. Cut the words everyone else's tool uses. "Spearheaded", "leveraged", "results-driven" and friends now read as template filler. Say what you did in plain language.
4. Tailor to the role, not to the tool. Mirror the posting's requirements with evidence from your own history, rather than pasting its phrasing back at the recruiter.
5. Read it aloud before you send it. If a sentence could sit on anyone's resume, rewrite it or delete it.
6. Keep one story per application. A shorter resume with three claims only you could make beats a long one that sounds mass-produced.
