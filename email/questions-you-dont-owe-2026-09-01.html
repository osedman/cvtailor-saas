You do not owe them that answer

A story did the rounds this week. A candidate was asked in an interview what they earned in their last job. They said, politely, that where they live employers are no longer allowed to ask that. The interviewer waved the objection away and pressed on. The room decided the law was optional.

It struck a nerve because everyone recognised the underlying assumption: that a candidate owes an answer to whatever an interviewer asks. You do not. Hiring is a negotiation between two parties, and one side has spent years accumulating rules, templates and screening tools while the other still treats every question as compulsory.

The other story worth knowing: a recruiter quoted a salary as "the minimum we start at." Two interviews later it turned out to be the theoretical maximum, bonuses included, in a perfect year. When challenged, she said the candidate had misunderstood and denied ever giving the figure. The candidate had it in an email. The denial collapsed on the spot. One saved message did what no amount of remembering could have.

What actually helps

Decline the salary history question

A growing list of places has banned it outright, and everywhere else you can simply not answer it. "I would rather focus on the range for this role" is a complete sentence, and any reasonable interviewer moves on. What you earned before is leverage against you and nothing else.

Every number that matters goes in writing

Base, bonus structure, the range, the start date. If it was said on a call, send a short follow-up email confirming what you heard and let them correct it. A misunderstanding cannot survive an email, which is exactly why you want one before you act on any figure.

Never resign on a verbal offer

Conditional means conditional. References, checks and the signed contract come first, then notice. The week of terror between quitting and clearing is entirely avoidable, and plenty of people this month learned that the hard way.

Brief your references

Do not let a reference be surprised by a phone call. Tell them the role, what you would like them to speak to, and roughly when to expect it. A good reference caught off guard is a mediocre reference, and you will never see the damage happen.

Read how they handle honesty

Disclose big commitments plainly at written-offer stage. A company that withdraws because you were straight with them about a long-planned trip is showing you what working there would be like, at the cheapest moment it ever will. That is information, not rejection.

Answer questions about what you can do. Decline the ones about what you cost.
