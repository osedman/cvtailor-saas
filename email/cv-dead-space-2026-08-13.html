The lines on your CV doing no work

The same handful of questions come up every single month. Do I include every college I attended. Where does willing to relocate go. How do I list two roles at the same company. Do I leave a gap if I was rehired five months later. Does my Etsy shop go on.

Underneath all of them sits one question nobody asks directly: does this line earn its space? It is worth asking, because most CVs are not too short. They are full of lines doing no work. A line earns its place only when it tells the reader something they could not already have assumed, and once you apply that test, a surprising amount of the page frees up for proof.

What actually helps

Cut "references available on request"

Nobody needs the promise. If they want references, they will ask. The line is worst when it sits alone at the bottom making the page look padded, or when it pushes out a stronger detail from your newest role. Keep a proper reference sheet in a separate document instead.

Describe volunteer work by what you were trusted with

People either hide unpaid work or paste in a big organisation name and hope. The name is rarely the useful part. "Volunteer, local food pantry" says almost nothing. "Coordinated the weekly pickup schedule for 12 volunteers" says a great deal. You do not need to inflate it, just name the responsibility.

Make certification status boringly clear

Where a role has a hard requirement, the reader is trying to settle one question fast: do you meet it right now? "PMP coursework" makes that harder. "PMP exam scheduled for September 2026" answers it. If something is expired, in progress or state specific, say so plainly rather than letting someone guess and move on.

Do not invent metrics you cannot stand behind

Plenty of good work simply does not produce numbers, and guessed figures fall apart the moment somebody asks about them. Where the numbers genuinely do not exist, describe the scope instead: how many, how often, and for whom.

Give every line the same test

Could the reader have assumed this without being told? If the answer is yes, that space belongs to something you can prove instead. Run the whole page through it once and see what survives.

One test for every line: could the reader have assumed it?
